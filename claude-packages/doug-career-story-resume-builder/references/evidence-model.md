# Career Evidence Model

Use this record for each item worth considering for a resume, LinkedIn profile, or interview story. Keep it in a table, document, or plain list according to the user’s preferred working style.

| Field | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Period and role | Approximate date range, formal title, and a useful scope note. |
| Context | The problem, mandate, or decision that created the work. |
| Contribution | What the candidate personally designed, decided, built, led, negotiated, analyzed, or changed. |
| Output | The concrete deliverable, process, event, recommendation, product, or decision. |
| Result or signal | A metric, adoption signal, decision made, risk avoided, feedback, or other observable effect. |
| Evidence | The specific artifact, source, or person who can confirm it. |
| Evidence basis | Documented, corroborated by a person, detailed first-person account, or assistant-derived lead. |
| Confidence | Confirmed, candidate, or needs confirmation. |
| Sensitivity | Public, internal-safe summary, or do not use externally. |
| Target relevance | Why this helps a particular role or professional narrative. |

## Turning a record into a claim

A resume claim should make one contribution legible without requiring a hiring reader to infer it. Use this sequence when the evidence supports it:

`Strong verb + work or decision + purpose or scope + supported result`

Example only:

`Built a repeatable intake process for cross-functional AI pilots, giving leaders a consistent record of intended use, risks, and lessons from each test.`

The result may be a concrete operating change rather than a number. Do not add a number simply because resume advice says one should appear.

## Evidence hierarchy

Strongest support usually comes from a finished work product, an official role record, performance feedback, a decision record, a published artifact, a named result, or a stakeholder who can confirm the work. A partial draft, chat conversation, calendar event, or personal recollection can surface a story, but it normally needs confirmation before it becomes a precise public claim.

When no outcome evidence exists, retain the contribution as a candidate item and ask a narrow question: what changed, who used it, what decision did it support, or who could confirm the value? That question is more useful than asking the user to estimate an impact percentage.

A detailed first-person account can still establish a credible contribution when older records do not exist. Record it honestly as such, capture the particulars, and pressure-test the claim against what the user would say if a former colleague asked for the real story. Assistant memory, a judgment profile, or a prior chat can identify a likely story or capability pattern. It is a lead until the user can anchor it in an example.
