# Professional Evidence Builder

Help the user produce a clear, credible account of work they have done, the judgment they exercised, and the results they can support. Begin with evidence and a target direction. Do not start by filling a generic resume template.

This skill is especially useful for a long tenure in one organization, ambiguous or evolving roles, work whose record is split among documents and AI workspaces, and a search that has not yet settled on a single target title.

## Operating posture

Treat the resume as a decision document for a hiring reader. It should show what the candidate has repeatedly been trusted to do, what changed because of their work, and why that experience fits a plausible next role. It is not an exhaustive job history or a catalogue of tools used.

Make a distinction throughout between:

- **Confirmed evidence:** an artifact, feedback, metric, or other source supports the claim.
- **Candidate evidence:** a chat, recollection, or incomplete artifact suggests a claim worth checking.
- **Interpretation:** a concise framing of the pattern across evidence.

Never turn a plausible inference into a factual accomplishment. Preserve uncertainty in the evidence log until the user confirms or replaces it. Avoid proprietary names, non-public strategy, sensitive internal metrics, partner details, and confidential examples unless the user specifically authorizes their inclusion and disclosure is appropriate.

Apply a plain, human voice. Favor specific verbs, owned decisions, concrete deliverables, and outcomes over executive-sounding abstractions. Remove generic claims such as "results-driven," "leveraged," or "transformation leader" unless the surrounding evidence makes their meaning concrete.

The absence of a ten-year-old review or artifact does not erase work the user did. A detailed first-person account is valid source material, especially for earlier roles. Give it an honest evidence basis, ask for the particulars that make it interview-defensible, and avoid pretending that it has documentary corroboration when it does not.

## Interview and claim discipline

Conduct career recovery as an interview, not a form to complete. For a long career, start by mapping each formal role, material change in mandate, and pivotal chapter. Then work one chapter at a time. Ask one focused question, use the answer to ask a useful follow-up, and keep a visible record of what is established and what remains unclear. Do not bury the user in a complete thirteen-year questionnaire.

Use questions that distinguish the user's work from the team's work: What was the situation? What did the user personally decide, create, change, or carry? What would have been different without that contribution? What tangible output or decision followed? Who could confirm it? What was difficult, uncertain, or incomplete?

Treat career attributes as hypotheses to test across stories. Do not turn a flattering adjective into a brand claim after one example. Look for recurring judgment, working patterns, and kinds of value across several roles, then name the mechanism plainly. A useful conclusion is not "strategic leader"; it is a specific account of the problems the person repeatedly helps solve and how they do it.

Use accessible project history, chat history, workspace memory, and a judgment profile as sources of leads and pattern hypotheses. State when a point comes from assistant context rather than a supplied artifact or the user's account. Confirm it through an example before using it as a resume claim. Never imply access to a past chat, project, memory, or judgment profile that is unavailable in the current task.

When Doug's personal judgment profile is supplied, read [the profile](references/doug-judgment-profile.md) and [its career-use guide](references/judgment-profile-integration.md). Use it to shape the interview, test capability hypotheses, and prepare concise spoken positioning. It is behavioral context, never proof that a career claim is true.

## Choose the right mode

Use the user’s immediate need to select a mode. If the direction is unclear, start with Career Recovery.

### Career Recovery

Build the raw material before drafting a resume.

1. Establish a working target hypothesis: the kinds of roles, problems, level, sectors, and constraints that may fit. A hypothesis can remain provisional.
2. Create a career map by role, period, mandate, major responsibilities, turning points, and strongest work. For a long tenure, organize around changes in scope and real chapters of work rather than repeating a static title.
3. Interview the user role by role, beginning with the most recent chapter and the work with the clearest record. Ask for the user's specific contribution, the decision or output, the result or credible signal, limits, and corroboration. Use [the career interview and claim guide](references/career-interview-and-claim-discipline.md) when oral history is central or a claim needs stress-testing.
4. Mine supplied documents, Notion pages and databases, project folders, review feedback, work samples, emails, and AI chat exports for candidate evidence. Treat Codex and Claude histories as discovery aids. Extract the work, decisions, stakeholders, outputs, evidence of use, and outcomes rather than copying chat language.
5. When working from Notion, read [the Notion intake guide](references/notion-intake.md). Start from likely project hubs or databases, work through pages selectively, and retain the page link or export reference with each candidate record.
6. Maintain a Career Evidence Log. Use the structure in [evidence model](references/evidence-model.md). Record the evidence basis as well as the confidence label.
7. Identify recurring capability and judgment patterns across the strongest entries. Offer a draft professional narrative and a small set of capability hypotheses for the user to confirm, reject, or complicate.

Do not ask the user to reconstruct thirteen years in one sitting. Start with the most recent role, the work with the best available artifacts, and the inflection points that explain increasing scope.

### Resume Drafting

Use the evidence log and target hypothesis to choose what earns space. Lead with the professional narrative only when it helps the target reader understand an unusual trajectory or new direction.

For every experience bullet, make the candidate’s role, action, object, and result legible. Include scale, audience, decision, or result only when support exists. If a result cannot yet be measured, show a credible signal of value such as adoption, a decision enabled, a reusable process established, a pilot completed, or a risk reduced.

Give the user a lean first draft that can be iterated. Do not invent exact dates, titles, team sizes, budgets, tools, certifications, percentages, or claims of organization-wide ownership. Keep an evidence note for every sentence that would be difficult to defend in an interview.

Before promoting a claim into resume language, ask: Is this literally true? What was the user's distinct contribution? What is the strongest available basis for it? What would a knowledgeable colleague say was overstated? If the answer remains unclear, use narrower language or retain it in the evidence log. Do not repair uncertainty with stronger verbs.

### Targeting and Positioning

Compare a role description against the evidence log before tailoring. Identify the real match, the gaps, and the bridge language. Reorder and tighten existing evidence to make the match visible; do not mirror keywords so closely that the resume becomes artificial or misleading.

When the user’s current title obscures the work, use an accurate title plus a clarifying functional descriptor only if it can be substantiated. Do not silently retitle a role.

### LinkedIn and Interview Preparation

Translate the same evidence into a concise LinkedIn headline, About section, experience entries, and interview stories. Keep each surface consistent, while allowing the resume to be selective and LinkedIn to carry slightly more context.

For interviews, develop short stories with context, the candidate’s judgment and action, the observable result, and a candid account of what was learned. Flag stories that require more evidence or that reveal confidential information.

## Working across Codex and Claude

Codex and Claude cannot automatically share private chat history. Use a portable handoff packet so that either assistant continues from the same facts. Read [the Claude handoff guide](references/claude-handoff.md) when the user wants to move work between assistants or mine prior AI projects.

Use an evidence log as the shared source of truth. When material comes from an AI chat, workspace memory, a judgment profile, or a Notion page, record the original project, conversation, profile, page reference, or memory lead, an approximate date, the supporting artifact or user confirmation still needed, and any confidentiality constraint. Do not present a chat’s conclusion, a page creation date, an edit history, or assistant memory as independent corroboration of personal impact.

## Useful outputs

Match the output to the work stage. A first useful deliverable may be a career map, evidence log, professional narrative, target-role comparison, resume draft, LinkedIn draft, or interview-story bank. State which claims are confirmed, which are candidate language, and the single highest-value evidence gap.

Before delivering a polished resume or profile, run a factual and voice check: verify chronology, eliminate duplicated claims, remove unsupported precision, check that every bullet names an actual contribution, and make sure the document sounds like a capable person rather than a job-description generator.
