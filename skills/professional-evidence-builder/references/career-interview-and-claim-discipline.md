# Career Interview and Claim Discipline

The purpose of the interview is to recover an accurate account of the user's working life, including roles for which no review, document, or project archive remains. It is not a cross-examination, and it is not a prompt to produce polished answers. Ask one question at a time, follow the useful thread, and name uncertainty plainly.

## Role recovery sequence

For each meaningful role or chapter, establish the mandate, the work that arrived with the role, the user's recurring responsibilities, the major changes in scope, and one or two consequential stories. Then ask: what did the user personally do? What did someone else own? What did the work produce? What changed, or what decision did it enable? What can the user point to, remember in detail, or ask a former colleague to confirm?

When the story is broad, ask for a representative episode. When it is polished, ask what made it hard or what did not work. When it credits a team, ask where the user's contribution began and ended. These questions make the account more credible; they do not diminish work that was genuinely collaborative.

## Claim strength ladder

Use the narrowest statement that is fully true. The language may become stronger only as the evidence supports it.

| Claim level | Appropriate when | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| Participated | The user was involved but cannot identify a distinct contribution. | Contributed to a cross-functional effort on [topic]. |
| Contributed | The user can name a concrete part of the work. | Developed the analysis that informed [decision]. |
| Led or owned | The user directed the work, held the decision, or was accountable for the outcome. | Led the design and delivery of [work]. |
| Produced a result | A supported outcome, adoption signal, decision, or changed practice exists. | Built [work], which was adopted by [audience] for [purpose]. |
| Caused a broader change | The causal relationship and scope are clear. | Established [practice] across [defined scope], reducing [supported risk or burden]. |

Avoid using a higher level merely because the lower one sounds modest. A precise contribution is more persuasive and more defensible than a broad claim of transformation.

## Capabilities and next-role fit

Build a capability hypothesis only after several stories point to the same pattern. State the pattern as a mechanism: the types of ambiguous problems the user handles, the information they make usable, the decisions they help others reach, and the conditions in which they do their best work.

Use a judgment profile to sharpen interview questions about standards, reasoning, communication, and decision-making. It may suggest a strength or a liability, but it cannot prove a career claim. Ask for an episode that confirms, refutes, or complicates the hypothesis.

At each stage, tell the user what is known, what rests on their account, what is an assistant-derived hypothesis, and the one question that would most improve the case.
